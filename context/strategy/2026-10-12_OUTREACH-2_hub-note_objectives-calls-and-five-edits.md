# OUTREACH-2 — what the two calls are for, how to have them, and five edits

## 1. What the calls are for (your reading, checked)
- **Now (October):** one or two households that say yes or maybe, so the hardware after the run has a home. PI-2 (Tue Oct 20) is the first household's Pi; the onboarding card is cut after the run passes (Oct 30 – Nov 2); the pilot starts late November. Nothing else this week depends on a yes.
- **December:** the first evidence you did not stage. Everything the company may say in public is a register row, and C-001..C-003 were all made on your rig. A week in another household mints C-004 (the packaged build runs a week in a house that is not yours) and C-005 (an automation fired there and the device confirmed it). COMMERCIAL-1's Dec 1–14 re-read is a read of those records.
- **Longer term — the pilot as the company's test method:** the weekly question ("did it do what you expected? if not, what did you expect?") turns a household's week into four kinds of product evidence: sentences the record can confirm (the only outward material the register admits); explain-surface gaps (a "why didn't it fire?" the page can't answer becomes an IR row); honest failures on hardware you did not pick; and the install measured in a house you don't live in. The calls are data too: the objections people raise (effort, privacy, risk, cost) are what the product has to answer before anyone pays for it.

Your reading is right, with one sharpening: the asks are less about finding testers than about getting the first records you didn't make. A household that tells you plainly about a bad week is worth more than an enthusiastic one.

## 2. How to have the calls
- **Who:** two homes that differ (a family and a single person or a couple; a house and an apartment), at least one person who is not technical, and people who will say plainly when something annoys them.
- **How:** the one logistics text (§2.0), then a call or a visit of 10–15 minutes. Lead with why you care (line 3: being able to see why things happened), then the gate (the run), then the ask (line 9) — then stop and listen. "I don't know yet" is a good answer; a maybe is a good result.
- **After a yes or a maybe:** ask what would make it easy, and what they already have (edit D). Bank one line per call (§5).
- **Not:** no name, no comparison, no promise beyond the script; nothing written to them before the receipt.

## 3. Five edits (A–E)
- **A — consent up front.** Line 8 promises a weekly five-minute check-in and "nothing else in the house changes", but the weekly copy of the record to your desk (§3, §4.3) is never said in the call. Put it in the main flow, at line 8: "…and once a week, with your OK, I copy the system's log to read how the week went." Consent before the yes, not after it.
- **B — drop "permanent" in line 3.** It promises more than a design with retention rules should, and said to a household "a permanent record of everything" sounds like surveillance. "It keeps a record of every command it sends and whether the device confirmed it" carries the point.
- **C — drop "through a formal verification chain" in line 4.** It means nothing to a household and reads as a claim. "I've had it running on real hardware for months; I update it like an app and it comes back up with everything working" stays inside C-001..C-003.
- **D — one question after a yes or a maybe:** "So I can plan the setup — what do you already have that's 'smart': bulbs, plugs, a doorbell, a thermostat? Which apps?" It is the device-coverage input your hardware question needs, asked as setup, not as a second ask.
- **E — the scope line, if they ask about risk:** "It only switches the few plugs we choose together — lamps and similar, nothing that heats; unplug the Pi and they're just normal plugs again." And in §3, your term: the pilot switches lamp-class loads only. It answers the risk question with a fact of the pilot's scope rather than a safety claim, and it limits what NEXSYS LLC is exposed to.

## 4. One thing for later (not this week)
Before install day (late November, after the receipt): a one-page pilot note for each household — what is installed, what is recorded, the weekly copy, how to stop — with a glance from counsel. The calls don't need it.
